# Anki Bot

Turns YouTube lecture videos into Anki flashcards.
Pipeline: YouTube URL → timestamped transcript → technical terms → up to 15 key ideas → cards → critic pass (Claude checks terms and cards against the card rules, fixing or cutting what breaks one) → .apkg (genanki).

## Card rules
Distilled from Andy Matuschak's "How to write good prompts" and Piotr Wozniak's "20 rules of formulating knowledge".

1. **One idea per card.** Never stitch several clauses into one answer, and never tuck a second fact into the question ("What did the treaty, drafted by Wilson, demand?" is two cards: who drafted it, and what it demanded). Extra details go on their own cards, or nowhere.
2. **Why/how over cloze.** Prefer genuine why/how questions. Use cloze only for bare facts like a term or a number. A famous quote is a cloze in this form: the quote with one key word blanked, then a dash, the speaker's name and the context in parentheses. "Give me liberty, or give me {{c1::death}}!" – Patrick Henry (urging Virginia to join the American Revolution).
3. **Sharp why/how.** A why-question needs a specific mechanism as its answer, not a vague one. "Why use bones in stock?" invites "they're cheap"; "How do bones give stock its rich texture?" doesn't.
4. **Precise.** The question must rule out other reasonable answers, so the answer is the same on every review, and short enough to be clearly right or wrong. So no questions of interpretation, like how a work portrays something, what it came to symbolize or how it shaped memory: the answer is an opinion that can be put many ways. Ask a fact instead, or drop the card.
5. **No yes/no questions.** Reframe them as open questions.
6. **No sets or lists as answers.** Never ask "What are the parts of X?" Split a list into one-blank cloze cards that keep the list in the same order.
7. **Cues narrow, never give away.** "(a type of function)" is fine; a rhyme or anything containing the answer is not.
8. **Minimum wording.** Cut every word that isn't needed to get the answer. No topic label in front of the card ("LLMs:", "Calculus:") and no setup wording: anything that isn't the question itself makes the card worse. The question names its own subject, so it still stands alone (rule 21). Keep the question short too: no background packed into it. "How did the printing press help spread the Reformation?", not "How did the printing press, invented by Gutenberg around 1440, help spread the Reformation?" If a question needs setup to make sense, the setup is its own card. Word questions plainly, the way a person would ask them out loud: "Which country did Napoleon invade in 1812?", not "Into which country's territory had Napoleon pushed in 1812?"
9. **Follow the lecture's story.** Main-idea cards walk through the lecture's main line of reasoning, one step per card, so together they read as a chain (a vaccine shows the body a harmless piece of a virus → the immune system makes antibodies against it → memory cells keep the recipe → so a real infection is beaten quickly). Few cards, no side angles.
10. **No trivia.** No cards on illustrative numbers (costs, counts, sizes, durations), on things mentioned only in passing, on the inner steps of a derivation or proof (ask what the result means, not why one step of getting there works), or about the speaker ("What analogy does the professor use…"). Ask about the idea itself. But in history and other fact-based subjects, the key people and places of the main story are core facts, not trivia: give them main-idea cards (short questions or fill-in-the-blanks). Dates get no cards unless the learner's note asks for them. Illustrative numbers, passing mentions and behind-the-scenes details (what inspired a painting, which weapon a soldier carried) still get no cards.
11. **Every technical word gets two vocab cards.** Any technical word the lecture uses, even a general one it doesn't explain (enzyme, protein, cell membrane, catalyst). Card one: "Catalyst" → its short definition. Card two: that definition → "Catalyst". Write both out as basic cards.
12. **Vocab is concepts, not names or basics.** No product, brand or company names (iPhone, Tylenol, Pfizer), and no people (key people of a history story get main-idea cards instead, rule 10), no units of storage (byte, gigabyte), no programming languages (C, Python), and no generic words like "state-of-the-art", "framework", "process", "factor" or "approximation". General words of the field (in machine learning: training set, evaluation, optimization) can stay; the user can cut them. Only terms people in the field actually use: a phrase the speaker coins for an idea ("the leaky bucket", "a ladder of thinking") gets a main-idea question instead, and nothing that appears only in one of the lecture's examples (a barcode, in an example about a shop), including the terms of a long worked example (down payment, closing costs and escrow, when a finance lecture works through one family's mortgage to teach compound interest). A term the lecture only name-drops or mentions in passing, without explaining it or using it in its reasoning (a chemistry intro listing "entropy" and "orbitals" as coming later), is not vocab either, unless it is a word of the subject or era that most people have never met (see the end of this rule). Notation the lecture reasons with is vocab: keep every symbol of the family it uses (p, q and r in a logic lecture, not just two of them). Skip words an educated adult already knows from the news and everyday reading (treaty, troops, invasion, ally, blockade, surrender), and words whose rough meaning is clear from context, where the exact meaning adds nothing (a ship's galley; sergeant vs corporal). In a technical subject, the field's own basic terms are what's being taught, so they stay even if familiar (enzyme and protein in a biology lecture, rule 11); in history and other story-driven subjects, the everyday words used to tell the story do not. Keep words specific to the subject or era that most people have never met (shogun, daimyo and ronin in a lecture on feudal Japan): they are what a learner most needs, and the user can cut them.
13. **Definitions hold no numbers or trivia.** No sizes, counts, costs or lecture-specific examples ("40 trials in the 2019 study"). Never define a term by its synonym (heart attack ↔ myocardial infarction); say what it is. A definition may name another term, but it must still say what the thing is or does: "sonnet" → "a poem of fourteen lines in iambic pentameter" is fine, but "iambic pentameter" → "the meter of a sonnet" is not, because it only points back. If the only honest definition just repeats the term's own words (steam engine → an engine powered by steam), the term is not vocab.
14. **Definitions are atomic.** One clause with the core meaning only: no "i.e.", no "e.g.", no parentheses, and nothing after a semicolon. Say what the thing means in plain words, not its formal construction or formula. Test: if you can cut the end of a definition and what's left is still a correct definition, cut it (tails often start with "which", "whose", "that" or "so"). Extra facts belong on their own cards, or nowhere. No full stop at the end.
15. **Important leftovers get one extra card.** If trimming a definition drops something important that no main-idea card covers, add one question card for it (not another vocab pair). Drop trivia, and drop anything a main-idea card already asks. Example: virus vs bacterium → "What can a bacterium do that a virus can't?" → "Reproduce on its own."
16. **No near-duplicate terms.** When two terms mean almost the same thing in this lecture (global warming and climate change; heart attack and myocardial infarction), keep only the one the lecture leans on.
17. **Main-idea answers are atomic too.** The shortest answer that is still right: drop secondary clauses and "and how…" add-ons. "By osmosis", not "By osmosis, and how fast depends on the concentration gradient; the membrane itself stays intact." No full stop at the end of an answer.
18. **Ask the general lesson, not the lecture's example.** "How do muscles grow stronger?" → "By repairing small tears after strain", not "How did the athletes in the 2019 study gain strength?" But keep the lecture's analogies: they make an idea easier to digest and remember. Ask about the analogy itself, not who said it: "How can we picture what a cell membrane does?" → "A bouncer at a club door", not "What analogy does the professor use?" A long worked example is different: it's a vehicle for teaching, not what the student needs to remember. When a lecture spends minutes working through one family's mortgage to introduce compound interest, the cards ask about compound interest, not about the mortgage.
19. **Contrasts name both sides.** "How does weather differ from climate?" → "Weather is the short-term state; climate is the long-term average."
20. **No overlapping cards.** Each card must test something no other card in the deck tests. Don't circle one idea from several angles ("the heart pumps blood", "the heart moves blood by squeezing", "the heart works like a water pump"): pick the single best card for it, and prefer the one with the lecture's analogy. No main-idea card may ask what a vocab term means or measures: its vocab card already tests that.
21. **Cards stand alone.** Cards get merged into larger decks later, so no card or definition may mention "the lecture", "the speaker" or "this video". Not "The lecture's description of the heart as…"; say what the thing is.
22. **Math is written in LaTeX.** Write every formula, symbol or variable between \( and \), the way Anki shows math: \(E = mc^2\), \(a_n\), \(\lambda\), \(\sqrt{2}\). Never plain-text stand-ins like "E = mc^2", "a_n" or a Greek letter's name spelled out. Inside \( \) goes only the math itself, no words. These markers aren't parentheses in the sense of rule 14. A fill-in-the-blank card blanks a whole \( \) expression, never part of one. Ideas named in words (gradient, eigenvalue) stay plain words.

### Examples
- Bad: "What are the characteristics of the Dead Sea?" (too broad). Good: "Where is the Dead Sea located?" → "On the border between Israel and Jordan."
- Bad: "Does chicken stock make vegetable dishes taste like chicken?" (yes/no). Good: an open question about how stock changes the flavor of vegetables.
- Bad: "What does GRE stand for in biochemistry?" Good: "GRE (biochemistry)" → "glucocorticoid response element."
- Bad definition: "An organelle that makes energy for the cell; often called the powerhouse of the cell." Good: "An organelle that makes energy for the cell."
- Bad definition: "The shape of a protein, i.e. how its chain of amino acids folds." Good: "The shape of a protein."
- Bad definition of standard deviation: "The square root of the average of the squared differences between each value and the mean." (a formula) Good: "How spread out a set of values is around their mean."
- Bad definition of inflation: "Prices going up." Good: "A general rise in prices that lowers what money can buy."
- Bad definition: "A gas (e.g. carbon dioxide) that traps heat in the atmosphere." Good: "A gas that traps heat in the atmosphere."

### A good main-idea set (for 3Blue1Brown's "But what is the Fourier Transform? A visual introduction")
Copy this style, not the content. The examples deliberately come from a lecture we don't test on, so they can't leak into a deck.
- "What does the Fourier transform do to a signal?" → "Splits it into the pure frequencies that make it up."
- "How can a signal's frequencies be found visually?" → "By winding its graph around a circle."
- "When a signal's graph is wound around a circle, what do we track as the winding frequency changes?" → "The center of mass of the wound-up graph."
- "Why does a wound-up graph's center of mass jump away from the middle at certain winding frequencies?" → "The winding matches a frequency in the signal, so its peaks all land on one side."
- "Plotting a wound-up graph's center of mass against the winding frequency gives an {{c1::almost-Fourier transform}}."
- "Why can the Fourier transform pull apart two notes played together?" → "It's linear: the transform of a sum is the sum of the transforms."
- "How does the real Fourier transform differ from a wound-up graph's center of mass?" → "The real transform takes the integral; the center of mass takes the average."

Cards cut from that lecture, and why:
- "How do you compute a Fourier transform in Python?" (a how-to procedure, not an idea)
- "What frequency is the A note in the example?" (number trivia)
- "How does the transform of a pure tone compare with a mixed one?" (asks for a comparison; ask why instead)
- Vocab terms: graph, data, state-of-the-art (generic words, not concepts of the subject)
