# Anki Bot

Turns YouTube lecture videos into Anki flashcards.
Pipeline: YouTube URL → timestamped transcript → technical terms → up to 15 key ideas → cards → critic pass (Claude checks terms and cards against the card rules, fixing or cutting what breaks one) → .apkg (genanki).

## Card rules
Distilled from Andy Matuschak's "How to write good prompts" and Piotr Wozniak's "20 rules of formulating knowledge".

1. **One idea per card.** Never stitch several clauses into one answer, and never tuck a second fact into the question ("What did the treaty, drafted by Wilson, demand?" is two cards: who drafted it, and what it demanded). Extra details go on their own cards, or nowhere.
2. **Why/how over cloze.** Prefer genuine why/how questions. Use cloze only for bare facts like a term or a number. A famous quote is a cloze in this form: the quote with one key word blanked, then a dash, the speaker's name and the context in parentheses. "Give me liberty, or give me {{c1::death}}!" – Patrick Henry (urging Virginia to join the American Revolution).
3. **Sharp why/how.** A why-question needs a specific mechanism as its answer, not a vague one. "Why use bones in stock?" invites "they're cheap"; "How do bones give stock its rich texture?" doesn't.
4. **Precise.** The question must rule out other reasonable answers, so the answer is the same on every review, and short enough to be clearly right or wrong. So no questions of interpretation, like how a work portrays something, what it came to symbolize or how it shaped memory: the answer is an opinion that can be put many ways. Ask a fact instead, or drop the card.
5. **No yes/no questions.** Reframe them as open questions.
6. **No sets or lists as answers.** Never ask "What are the parts of X?" Split a list into one-blank cloze cards that keep the list in the same order.
7. **Cues narrow, never give away.** "(a type of function)" is fine; a rhyme or anything containing the answer is not.
8. **Minimum wording.** Cut every word that isn't needed to get the answer. No topic label in front of the card ("LLMs:", "Calculus:") and no setup wording: anything that isn't the question itself makes the card worse. The question names its own subject, so it still stands alone (rule 21). Keep the question short too: no background packed into it. "How did the printing press help spread the Reformation?", not "How did the printing press, invented by Gutenberg around 1440, help spread the Reformation?" If a question needs setup to make sense, the setup is its own card. Word questions plainly, the way a person would ask them out loud: "Which country did Napoleon invade in 1812?", not "Into which country's territory had Napoleon pushed in 1812?"
9. **Follow the lecture's story.** Main-idea cards walk through the lecture's main line of reasoning, one step per card, so together they read as a chain (training is like compressing the internet → done on a GPU cluster → the result is like a zip file → but lossy). Few cards, no side angles.
10. **No trivia.** No cards on illustrative numbers (costs, counts, sizes, durations), on things mentioned only in passing, on the inner steps of a derivation or proof (ask what the result means, not why one step of getting there works), or about the speaker ("What analogy does Karpathy use…"). Ask about the idea itself. But in history and other fact-based subjects, the key people and places of the main story are core facts, not trivia: give them main-idea cards (short questions or fill-in-the-blanks). Dates get no cards unless the learner's note asks for them. Illustrative numbers, passing mentions and behind-the-scenes details (what inspired a painting, which weapon a soldier carried) still get no cards.
11. **Every technical word gets two vocab cards.** Any technical word the lecture uses, even a general one it doesn't explain (GPU, zip file, neural network, compile, binary). Card one: "Lossy compression" → its short definition. Card two: that definition → "Lossy compression". Write both out as basic cards.
12. **Vocab is concepts, not names or basics.** No product, model or company names (Llama 2, ChatGPT, Claude), and no people (key people of a history story get main-idea cards instead, rule 10), no units of storage (byte, gigabyte), no programming languages (C, Python), and no generic words like "state-of-the-art", "data type", "dependencies", "training run", "compression ratio", "objective" or "approximation". General machine-learning words (training set, evaluation, optimization) can stay; the user can cut them. Only terms people in the field actually use: a phrase the speaker coins for an idea ("inscrutable artifact", "dreaming") gets a main-idea question instead, and nothing that appears only in one of the lecture's examples (ISBN), including the terms of a long worked example (pi, radius, circumference and concentric rings, when a calculus lecture works through the area of a circle). A term the lecture only name-drops or mentions in passing, without explaining it or using it in its reasoning (a calculus intro listing "chain rule" and "Taylor series" as coming later), is not vocab either. Notation the lecture reasons with is vocab: keep every symbol of the family it uses (dx, dr and dA, not just two of them). Skip words an educated adult already knows from the news and everyday reading (treaty, troops, invasion, ally, blockade, surrender), and words whose rough meaning is clear from context, where the exact meaning adds nothing (a horse's trot; platoon vs company). In a technical subject, the field's own basic terms are what's being taught, so they stay even if familiar (GPU and zip file in a lecture on how LLMs work, rule 11); in history and other story-driven subjects, the everyday words used to tell the story do not. Keep words specific to the subject or era that most people have never met (shogun, daimyo and ronin in a lecture on feudal Japan): they are what a learner most needs, and the user can cut them.
13. **Definitions hold no numbers or trivia.** No sizes, counts, costs or lecture-specific examples ("140 GB for Llama 2 70B"). Never define a term by its synonym (parameters ↔ weights); say what it is. A definition may name another term, but it must still say what the thing is or does: "sonnet" → "a poem of fourteen lines in iambic pentameter" is fine, but "iambic pentameter" → "the meter of a sonnet" is not, because it only points back. If the only honest definition just repeats the term's own words (rifle regiment → a regiment of riflemen), the term is not vocab.
14. **Definitions are atomic.** One clause with the core meaning only: no "i.e.", no "e.g.", no parentheses, and nothing after a semicolon. Say what the thing means in plain words, not its formal construction or formula. Test: if you can cut the end of a definition and what's left is still a correct definition, cut it (tails often start with "which", "whose", "that" or "so"). Extra facts belong on their own cards, or nowhere. No full stop at the end.
15. **Important leftovers get one extra card.** If trimming a definition drops something important that no main-idea card covers, add one question card for it (not another vocab pair). Drop trivia, and drop anything a main-idea card already asks. Example: open-source vs open-weights → "What does an open-source model release that an open-weights model doesn't?" → "Its training details and data."
16. **No near-duplicate terms.** When two terms mean almost the same thing in this lecture (open-source model and open-weights model; language model and LLM), keep only the one the lecture leans on.
17. **Main-idea answers are atomic too.** The shortest answer that is still right: drop secondary clauses and "and how…" add-ons. "In the parameter values", not "In the parameter values and how they are obtained; the architecture is fully understood." No full stop at the end of an answer.
18. **Ask the general lesson, not the lecture's example.** "How do models improve?" → "By scaling data and compute", not "How do state-of-the-art models get beyond Llama 2?" But keep the lecture's analogies: they make an idea easier to digest and remember. Ask about the analogy itself, not who said it: "How can we picture the result of training?" → "A zip file of the internet", not "What analogy does Karpathy use?" A long worked example is different: it's a vehicle for teaching, not what the student needs to remember. When a lecture spends minutes deriving the area of a circle to introduce integrals, the cards ask about integrals and derivatives, not about the circle.
19. **Contrasts name both sides.** "How does training's compression differ from a zip file?" → "Training's compression is lossy; a zip file is lossless."
20. **No overlapping cards.** Each card must test something no other card in the deck tests. Don't circle one idea from several angles ("we don't understand the parameters", "LLMs are inscrutable artifacts", "unlike a car, we don't understand the parts"): pick the single best card for it, and prefer the one with the lecture's analogy. No main-idea card may ask what a vocab term means or measures: its vocab card already tests that.
21. **Cards stand alone.** Cards get merged into larger decks later, so no card or definition may mention "the lecture", "the speaker" or "this video". Not "The lecture's description of LLMs as…"; say what the thing is.

### Examples
- Bad: "What are the characteristics of the Dead Sea?" (too broad). Good: "Where is the Dead Sea located?" → "On the border between Israel and Jordan."
- Bad: "Does chicken stock make vegetable dishes taste like chicken?" (yes/no). Good: an open question about how stock changes the flavor of vegetables.
- Bad: "What does GRE stand for in biochemistry?" Good: "GRE (biochemistry)" → "glucocorticoid response element."
- Bad definition: "Neural network trained on huge amounts of text to generate text; in essence a parameters file plus code that runs it." Good: "A neural network trained on huge amounts of text to generate text."
- Bad definition: "The structure of a neural network, i.e. how its computations are arranged." Good: "The structure of a neural network."
- Bad definition of standard deviation: "The square root of the average of the squared differences between each value and the mean." (a formula) Good: "How spread out a set of values is around their mean."
- Bad definition of parroting: "Reproducing training documents verbatim." Good: "Generating statistically likely text by mimicking patterns from training data without genuine comprehension."
- Bad definition: "Small program (e.g. in C) that runs the neural network using the parameters." Good: "Small program that runs the neural network using the parameters."

### A good main-idea set (for 3Blue1Brown's "But what is the Fourier Transform? A visual introduction")
Copy this style, not the content. The examples deliberately come from a lecture we don't test on, so they can't leak into a deck.
- "What does the Fourier transform do to a signal?" → "Splits it into the pure frequencies that make it up."
- "How can a signal's frequencies be found visually?" → "By winding its graph around a circle."
- "When a signal's graph is wound around a circle, what do we track as the winding frequency changes?" → "The center of mass of the wound-up graph."
- "Why does a wound-up graph's center of mass jump away from the middle at certain winding frequencies?" → "The winding matches a frequency in the signal, so its peaks all land on one side."
- "Plotting a wound-up graph's center of mass against the winding frequency gives an {{c1::almost-Fourier transform}}."
- "Why can the Fourier transform pull apart two notes played together?" → "It's linear: the transform of a sum is the sum of the transforms."
- "How does the real Fourier transform differ from a wound-up graph's center of mass?" → "The real transform takes the integral; the center of mass takes the average."

Cards cut from that lecture, and why:
- "How do you compute a Fourier transform in Python?" (a how-to procedure, not an idea)
- "What frequency is the A note in the example?" (number trivia)
- "How does the transform of a pure tone compare with a mixed one?" (asks for a comparison; ask why instead)
- Vocab terms: graph, data, state-of-the-art (generic words, not concepts of the subject)
